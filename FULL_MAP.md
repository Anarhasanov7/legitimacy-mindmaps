#### "Legitimacy makes going abroad pay off" ✅ POSITIVE
- **Paper:** Ghods — PhD thesis, Aix-Marseille University 2021 (supervised by Aldebert & Ricard; Bitektine on the jury)
- *The question:* Expanding abroad is risky ("liability of foreignness") — does legitimacy decide whether it pays off?
- *What they did:* Tracked 67 French biotech SMEs over 8 years using "fixed-effects panel" — statistics that compare each firm to its OWN past, so stable firm traits can't fake the effect
- *How legitimacy was scored:* a composite index built from observable proxies — certifications, partnerships, media coverage — combined into one legitimacy score per firm per year, then tested against internationalization and performance
- *Sample:* 412 firm-year observations · France · 2010–18
- *Found:* legitimacy moderates — internationalization only helps performance when legitimacy is high
- *Caveats → solutions:*
  - *Problem: small niche sample* — 67 firms, biotech only → *Fix:* 412 observations over time + fixed effects absorb unobserved firm differences
- *For you:* atEnA is cross-border France–Italy — legitimacy is what makes border-crossing pay off

#### "What 'legitimate' means changes by country" ✅ POSITIVE
- **Paper:** Ghods, Ricard & Aldebert — E&RD 2024
- *The question:* Do founders in different contexts picture legitimacy differently? Everyone assumed universal — never tested
- *What they did:* 150 French entrepreneurs wrote the first words that come to mind for "legitimate entrepreneur" + ranked importance; a matrix (how often × how important) separates core beliefs from peripheral ones
- *Sample:* 75 domestic vs 75 international entrepreneurs · France · 2019–21
- *Found:* domestic and international founders hold different mental models of legitimacy
- *Caveats → solutions:*
  - *Problem: grouping free words is subjective* — different researchers could group differently → *Fix:* lemmatization (grouping same-meaning words by rule, not opinion) + two independent coders agreeing (kappa > .85 = strong agreement)
- *For you:* France vs Italy must be a formal moderator, not pooled data

#### "Legitimacy can be scored from texts" ✅ POSITIVE — predicts failure
- **Paper:** Ghods, Ricard, Aldebert & Schoon — British Journal of Management 2026
- *The question:* Legitimacy was only measurable via surveys — can an algorithm score it from venture texts at scale?
- *What they did:* NLP (a language algorithm) scored 524 venture press releases against legitimacy dictionaries; checked whether scores predict real 5-year survival
- *Found:* yes — text scores predict which ventures die
- *Caveats → solutions:*
  - *Problem: the algorithm may misread promotional language* → *Fix:* human experts calibrated the dictionary + scores validated against actual firm death

## 3. SIRAZ — the judges (who decides if you look real)
- **What she did:** showed ordinary people fake startup profiles (like fake job applications) and asked "how legitimate does this look?"
- **What she measured:** the legitimacy rating each judge gave each profile — one number per profile, thousands of ratings total
- **What she found:** looking like a NORMAL, recognizable business beats every fancy signal (endorsements, certifications). And judges punish women / immigrant / minority founders harder after identical failures.
- **The catch:** judges rated fake profiles — nobody risked real money → it measures what people SAY, not what they'd DO
- **Why it matters to you:** she proved WHICH signals judges care about → your training teaches exactly those signals
### "Looking normal beats endorsements" ✅ POSITIVE — ×2, n=1,866
- **Paper:** Thaler, Sievert, Siraz, Claes & Pinz — Journal of Management Studies (online first)
- *The question:* When a judge sees a new venture, which signal convinces them most — looking like a normal business, having official authorization, or having endorsements?
- *What they did:* Showed judges made-up venture profiles where each signal was switched on/off
- *What "normal business" consisted of (the 3 signals tested):*
  - CATEGORICAL FIT = description matches a known business type (a bakery selling bread) vs a hybrid that fits nothing — THIS is "looking normal"
  - AUTHORIZATION = official license/permit — the state says it's real
  - ENDORSEMENT = a respected org publicly vouches for it
- *What they measured:* the legitimacy score (propriety rating) each judge gave each profile — a 1–7 scale of "is this appropriate / does this look like a real business"
- *Sample:* 1,866 judges across 2 separate experiments · online panels · Germany/UK · 2022–23
- *Found:* "Fitting the category of a real business" dominated — endorsements added almost nothing on top
- *Caveats → solutions:*
  - *Problem:* fake profiles ≠ real decisions → *Fix:* realistic pitch summaries + attention checks
  - *Problem:* could be a fluke → *Fix:* ran twice, same result
- *For you:* teach founders to LOOK like a recognizable business first — your Cognitive capability is the strongest lever

### "Mixed signals stall judges" ✅ CONFIRMED
- **Paper:** Siraz, Claes & De Castro — European Management Review 2026
- *The question:* What happens when a venture sends good AND bad signals at the same time?
- *What they did:* Each judge rated 16 made-up venture profiles mixing positive and negative cues. (This design is called a "conjoint" — you show many combinations and see which signals move the rating.)
- *4 cue types mixed:* REGULATORY (permits) · PRAGMATIC (revenue, clients) · NORMATIVE (values, CSR) · COGNITIVE (familiar category) — each + or −, e.g. "licensed but losing clients"
- *Sample:* 247 judges × 16 profiles each = 3,952 ratings · France/Spain · 2021–23 · topic: fracking
- *Found:* contradictory cues cancel each other → judgment stalls in the grey zone; judges' own green values made them discount pro-industry signals
- *Caveats → solutions:*
  - *Problem:* judges get tired → *Fix:* randomized profiles, only 16 each
  - *Problem:* judges' own beliefs interfere → *Fix:* measured their values first, checked if green judges rated differently (a "moderator")
- *For you:* teach consistent signal bundles — one contradiction stalls the whole judgment

### "Defending a hated product backfires" ❌ NEGATIVE
- **Paper:** Claes, Siraz, De Castro & Lapeyre — European Management Review 2025
- *The question:* Producers believe their justifications work — but do audiences agree?
- *What they did:* First interviewed 12 foie gras producers to collect the 5 arguments the industry actually uses; then had judges rate 32 made-up profiles built from those arguments
- *5 real defenses tested:* "it's tradition" · "it creates jobs" · "animals are well treated" — judges rated the STRATEGY, not the product
- *Sample:* 31 judges × 32 profiles = 992 ratings · France · 2019–22
- *Found:* strategies the industry loves were judged inappropriate → they actively HURT legitimacy
- *Caveats → solutions:*
  - *Problem:* only 31 judges → *Fix:* 992 total ratings + real industry interviews
  - *Problem:* niche industry → *Fix:* triangulated with fieldwork
- *For you:* never assume a trained signal works — the audience decides if it's appropriate; you must test it

### "Women & immigrants punished harder after failure" ✅ CONFIRMED — bias
- **Paper:** Siraz et al. — Academy of Management / BCERC 2020
- *The question:* Same failure, different founder — does the judge's verdict change with gender or origin?
- *What they did:* Judges rated failed-venture profiles where founder gender, immigrant status, education, and failure cause were shuffled randomly
- *4 things varied:* GENDER · IMMIGRANT status · EDUCATION · WHY it failed — everything else identical → any difference = founder bias
- *Sample:* 74 judges × 32 profiles = 2,368 ratings · Europe · 2018–19
- *Found:* women and immigrants penalized harder for identical failures; education helped them less
- *Caveats → solutions:*
  - *Problem:* judges hide bias → *Fix:* profiles vary on 4 dimensions at once, so nobody guesses the topic
- *For you:* run subgroup analyses in ATENA — does training help minority founders equally?

### "Same finances, lower score for Latinx name" ✅ CONFIRMED — bias
- **Paper:** Siraz, Claes & Alvarez — Journal of Business Ethics 2026
- *The question:* Is a Latinx founder judged worse just because of the surname — even with identical finances?
- *What they did:* Same conjoint trick — surname varied (Latinx vs Anglo) while debt, revenue, and track record stayed identical
- *Varied per profile:* SURNAME (Latinx/Anglo) · DEBT · REVENUE · TRACK RECORD — only the name changed
- *Sample:* 781 US judges × 16 profiles = 12,496 ratings · 2021–24 · half the judges were Latinx themselves
- *Found:* Latinx founders scored lower even at equal/lower debt — and Latinx judges were NOT more lenient → the bias is in the system
- *Caveats → solutions:*
  - *Problem:* surname might signal risk → *Fix:* finances literally identical, only the name changed

### ⤷ THE 5 STATES — explained in plain words
*Siraz, Claes, De Castro & Vaara (2023), Journal of Management Studies*

#### 1. LEGITIMACY — "Everyone just accepts it" ✅
- **In plain words:** Nobody even questions it. It looks so normal that judges don't stop to evaluate it.
- **Everyday example:** A bakery opening on your street. Nobody asks "is this a real business?" — it just IS one.
- **Technical:** favorable judgments · strong congruence with norms · passive (taken-for-granted) processing
- **Strategies:** Framing, Mythopoesis, Normalization — keep the buffer, don't draw attention
- **Warning:** Defending a fully-legitimate venture too loudly can BACKFIRE — it makes people look for problems

#### 2. CONDITIONAL LEGITIMACY — "Legitimate IF you follow the rules" ⚠️
- **In plain words:** "Yes, BUT..." — judges accept you only if you meet specific conditions.
- **Everyday example:** GMO food is accepted ONLY with labeling. A drone startup is fine ONLY with flight permits. Break the condition → acceptance collapses.
- **Technical:** acceptance conditional on compliance · opposition is ACTIVE, ready to pounce if you breach · evaluators scrutinize you
- **Strategies:** Authorization, Rationalization, Self-Regulation, Commitment Narratives

#### 3. UNKNOWN LEGITIMACY — "The judge can't decide" ❓ ← WHERE YOUR FOUNDERS START
*The judge has no stable verdict — but WHY can't they decide? Three different reasons = three sub-types:*
##### A. UNDETERMINED — "Nobody is even looking"
- **In plain words:** The venture is invisible. Judges haven't thought about it at all — not because they dislike it, they just never noticed.
- **Everyday example:** A founder working from their bedroom that nobody has heard of. No opinion exists yet — good or bad.
- **The problem to fix:** ZERO ATTENTION. You need people to notice you exist.
- **Strategy:** emotional framing, high-visibility storytelling → build baseline salience (get on the radar)

##### B. UNDECIDED — "They look, but can't make up their mind" ← YOUR ATENA FOUNDERS
- **In plain words:** Judges DO look, but the picture is blurry — missing information, mixed signals, or no clear category. They literally cannot judge yet.
- **Everyday example:** A woman who left a marketing job to launch a "wellness-tech" venture — is it a health company? a tech company? a coaching service? The judge sees it but can't place it.
- **The problem to fix:** CONFUSION, not invisibility. The judge needs a recognizable frame.
- **Strategy:** analogical reasoning — "it's like X but for Y" (frame the unknown as familiar) · role models · authorized intermediaries (a trusted program vouching)
- **Direct connection to you:** ATENA's 12h module IS the analogical-framing mechanism — it teaches founders to give judges a familiar category to latch onto

##### C. POLARIZED — "Half love it, half hate it"
- **In plain words:** The audience itself is split into two camps with opposite verdicts, roughly balanced.
- **Everyday example:** Crypto startups — some judges see "the future of finance," others see "a scam." Same venture, two stable opposite judgments.
- **The problem to fix:** A DIVIDED audience — you can't win everyone with one story.
- **Strategy:** bridging narratives, dual-framing — different framing for each audience segment

#### 4. CONDITIONAL ILLEGITIMACY — "Illegitimate, but tolerated anyway" ⚠️
- **In plain words:** Judges think it's improper IN PRINCIPLE — but allow it anyway because it serves a necessary purpose.
- **Everyday example:** Medical cannabis. Morally contested, but tolerated because patients need it. Or a payday lender — distasteful, but legal and filling a gap.
- **Technical:** fundamentally incongruent BUT saved by pragmatic necessity or a regulatory carve-out · opposition actively fights the exception
- **Strategies:** Authorized Exception (formal dispensation), Higher-Good Moralization ("yes it's ugly, but it serves a greater good")

#### 5. ILLEGITIMACY — "Actively rejected" ❌
- **In plain words:** Judges have decided: this should not exist. It's rejected, sanctioned, or stigmatized.
- **Everyday example:** A scam "investment scheme" — not just distrusted, but shut down and condemned.
- **Technical:** unfavorable, sticky judgments · severe incongruence with societal norms · rejection is ritualized/passive — it no longer needs justification
- **Strategies:** impression management, decoupling, normalization accounts, or relocate to a favorable jurisdiction

#### HOW STATES FLIP (when the rules change)
- **Trigger:** Venture enters a new environment or market
- **Determinants:** Stability of Reference Framework × Presence of Type Conflicts × Strength of Societal NVBs
- **Key Insight:** A venture's legitimacy state can flip from legitimate to contested without any change in its own behavior, solely because the societal yardstick shifted

##### A1. FIELD EXPERIMENT — proves the cause (Article 1)
- **1. Who & What:** Nascent entrepreneurs randomly split into Treatment (12h module) vs Control. You measure the founder-side of the dyad + objective milestones.
- **2. How:** Random assignment → T0 baseline → 12h module → T1 post-test → T4 follow-up.
    - *The steps, in order ↓*
      - Step a — Recruit ATENA founders → collect T0 baseline from EVERYONE before anyone knows their group
        - ⚠ can go wrong: people who signed up are already motivated → Fix: report baseline table for both groups to show they started equal
      - Step b — Randomize into treatment / control (the fair coin flip)
        - ⚠ can go wrong: someone swaps or complains → Fix: assignment is locked before the module is announced
      - Step c — Separate the cohorts (different slots / rooms)
        - ⚠ can go wrong: they meet anyway at the program → Fix: measure the leak — ask control "what did you hear about the module?"
      - Step d — Deliver the 12h module to treatment only
        - ⚠ can go wrong: some treated don't attend → Fix: ITT keeps them counted anyway; attendance rate feeds the TOT number
      - Step e — Survey both arms at T1 and T4 + collect milestones

- **Problem: small sample / power**
  - What it means: too few founders → random noise can hide a real effect → "no effect" even if training works
  - How big: HIGH — the #1 attack on field experiments
  - Fix 1 — power analysis
    - What it is: a calculation you do BEFORE the study that answers "how many founders do I need?"
    - How it works: you guess the effect size you expect (e.g., medium) → plug in how sure you want to be (usually 80%) → the formula returns the minimum n → you only start once you have it
    - What it solves: turns "your sample is too small" from a debate into a number you already defended upfront
  - Fix 2 — ITT (intention-to-treat)
    - What it is: a rule for who counts in your results
    - How it works: everyone who was randomly assigned stays in their group for analysis — even the founders who skipped the training. You compare "everyone offered training" vs "everyone not offered"
    - Why it works: if you only counted attendees, motivated people would pile up on one side → the comparison breaks. Keeping everyone in their original group preserves the fair coin flip
    - What it solves: dropouts and skippers can't silently break your randomization
  - Fix 3 — effect size + confidence interval
    - What it is: instead of just "yes/no significant", you report how BIG the change is and the range of plausible values
    - How it works: effect size = the gap between groups in plain units (e.g., +0.4 on a 7-point scale); the CI = the honest band around it ("the true effect is probably between X and Y")
    - What it solves: with a small sample you can still show a meaningful effect and be honest about uncertainty — instead of a bare "p > .05, nothing found"
- **Problem: attrition (founders drop out)**
  - What it means: some founders leave between T0→T1→T4 → if quitters differ from stayers, the groups stop being comparable → silent bias
  - How big: HIGH — most common field-RCT killer
  - Fix 1 — leaver/stayer comparison
    - What it is: a check you run, not a fix that prevents dropout
    - How it works: take the T0 baseline answers of everyone who later quit → compare them to the stayers → if they look the same (age, confidence, venture stage), dropout was random noise, not systematic
    - What it solves: turns "your dropouts ruined it" into "I showed the leavers looked identical to stayers"
  - Fix 2 — multiple imputation
    - What it is: a way to handle the empty cells instead of deleting those founders
    - How it works: for a founder who quit at T4, the model uses everything known about them (T0 answers, group, progress) to generate several plausible T4 answers → runs the analysis on each → combines results
    - What it solves: keeps everyone in the analysis AND keeps honest uncertainty — better than either deleting people or pretending nothing is missing
  - What remains: imputation assumes missingness is explainable by what you already observed — you state this assumption openly
- **Problem: contamination (control learns the content)**
  - What it means: treated founders share module content with control peers → the control group gets secretly treated → gap shrinks artificially
  - How big: MEDIUM-HIGH — same program = they talk
  - Fix 1 — cohort separation
    - What it is: keep the two groups physically apart
    - How it works: treatment and control run in different time slots / cohorts → they never meet → no channel for the content to leak
    - What it solves: removes the leak path entirely rather than hoping nobody talks
  - Fix 2 — staggered rollout
    - What it is: control isn't "no training" — it's "training LATER"
    - How it works: control founders get the module AFTER T4 → their T0–T4 answers are still clean → and you keep everyone happy (nobody is denied training forever)
    - What it solves: gives a clean untreated comparison period without creating angry "never-treated" founders
  - Fix 3 — measure the leak directly
    - What it is: ask, don't guess
    - How it works: at T1/T4 ask control founders "did you hear about the module content? what did you hear?" → you quantify how much spillover happened instead of assuming zero
    - What it solves: if some leak happened anyway, you can report its size instead of hiding it
- **Problem: self-report only**
  - What it means: asking "do you feel legitimate?" measures confidence inside the head — not what judges outside actually think
  - How big: MEDIUM
  - Fix 1 — objective milestones
    - What it is: count real-world behavior, not just feelings
    - How it works: track things founders actually DO — registering the business, submitting funding applications, getting first revenue — these are hard to fake on a survey
    - What it solves: proves the training changed behavior, not just self-image

- **Problem: CMV (common-method variance)**
  - What it means: you measure cause AND effect on the same questionnaire in the same sitting → the two correlate because of HOW you asked (same scale, same mood, same person), not because of reality
  - Everyday version: ask someone "do you feel confident?" then "do you feel legitimate?" in one breath — a cheerful person says yes to both → fake correlation
  - How big: MEDIUM
  - Fix 1 — temporal separation
    - What it is: measure different things at different times
    - How it works: self-legitimacy at T1, outcomes at T4 → the same sitting can't fake a link measured weeks apart
    - What it solves: the correlation can't be just a moment's mood
  - Fix 2 — different question formats
    - What it is: mix up scales and wording so "answering style" can't drive the result
    - How it works: if every question uses the same 1–7 agree/disagree format, people slip into a pattern → varying it breaks the pattern
    - What it solves: correlation reflects real difference, not a habit of clicking "4"
  - Fix 3 — objective markers
    - What it is: anchor answers to things outside the questionnaire
    - How it works: compare survey answers against real milestones + the pitch-deck text score → a self-report backed by observable behavior can't be pure answering-style
    - What it solves: gives the survey an outside reality check
- **Problem: panel conditioning**
  - What it means: filling in the same survey repeatedly makes founders MORE aware of legitimacy — the measurement itself becomes a mini-treatment
  - Everyday version: weigh yourself daily → you start dieting → the scale changed the behavior it was only supposed to measure
  - How big: MEDIUM
  - Fix — control group fills the same survey
    - What it is: the comparison group also answers at T0, T1, T4
    - How it works: if the survey itself teaches awareness, it teaches BOTH groups equally → when you compare groups, the survey effect is identical on both sides → it cancels out
    - What it solves: whatever the survey does to people, it does to everyone → the training difference stays clean
- **Problem: maturation**
  - What it means: founders improve naturally over time — business skills grow with experience → any change isn't necessarily caused by training
  - How big: HIGH without a control group, near-zero with it
  - Fix — randomized control group
    - What it is: a group that goes through the same months WITHOUT the module
    - How it works: both groups mature at the same natural rate → control group's improvement = "what time alone does" → treated group's EXTRA improvement beyond that = the training effect
    - What it solves: "they just got better with time" → "the treated group improved MORE than time alone explains

#### THE MATH CHEAT SHEET — formulas for the board
*Each = formula + plain-English meaning + the number that matters for you*
##### Cohen's d — the effect size
- **Formula:** d = (mean_treatment − mean_control) / pooled standard deviation
- **Plain:** "how many standard-deviations apart are the two groups?" — d=0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large
- **For you:** McKenzie's 10% profit effect ≈ small-to-medium d → you need power for d≈0.3–0.4
##### Power analysis — the sample-size formula
- **Rule of thumb:** n per group ≈ 16 / d² for 80% power at p<.05
- **Plain:** medium effect (d=0.4) → ~100 founders/group; small effect (d=0.2) → ~400/group
- **For you:** this is why the power analysis must run BEFORE data collection — the formula sets the recruitment target
##### ITT vs TOT — the Wald estimator
- **Formula:** TOT = ITT / compliance rate
- **Plain:** if ITT effect = +0.10 and only 70% of treated actually attended → the per-attendee effect (TOT) ≈ 0.14
- **For you:** lets you report BOTH the policy effect (ITT: offering training) and the dose effect (TOT: attending training)
##### Difference-in-Differences (DiD)
- **Formula:** (T1−T0 of treatment) − (T1−T0 of control)
- **Plain:** "extra change beyond what time alone does" — subtracts maturation automatically
- **For you:** your T0→T1→T4 design is built exactly for this subtraction
##### Cohen's kappa — inter-rater agreement
- **Formula:** κ = (observed agreement − chance agreement) / (1 − chance agreement)
- **Plain:** agreement AFTER removing what random guessing would produce — κ>.80 = strong, >.60 = acceptable
- **For you:** the number that turns "you coded it yourself" into "two independent coders agreed at κ=?"
##### AMCE — how conjoint effects are read (Siraz's method)
- **Plain:** the average change in the rating when ONE cue switches on while all others stay randomized — lets her rank signals (categorical fit >> endorsement) without confounds
- **For you:** your Article 4 pitch-experiment will report the same AMCEs — same grammar as Siraz, real bank officers as judges

### 12 TOUGH QUESTIONS — with my answers
*Structured for rapid 30-second mastery: Model Answer (Core Claim + Two-Link Logic) → Backing Literature*

#### A. THE GAP — "isn’t it just nobody did this before?"

##### Q1: "Isn't your gap merely an absence of literature ('nobody did an RCT')?"
- **Model Answer:** No. It is a fundamental theoretical tension. Legitimacy is defined as an evaluation that resides strictly in the external stakeholder's mind, whereas entrepreneurship training operates strictly inside the founder. Existing theory cannot predict whether altering a founder's internal knowledge and signals will successfully bridge the boundary and change an external evaluator's proprietary judgment.
- **Backing Literature:** Mark Suchman (1995 AMR); Eric Schoon (2022 ASR); Sonia Siraz et al. (2023 JMS).

##### Q2: "Why do you assume your novice founders start in 'Unknown' legitimacy rather than Illegitimacy?"
- **Model Answer:** Following Siraz et al. (2023), unknown legitimacy includes the *undecided* sub-state, where evaluators lack sufficient information to judge. Novice, unconventional founders possess no negative track record or stigma—they simply suffer from a lack of visibility and recognizable category fit. We do not assume this as a proven fact; we treat it as a theoretical expectation and empirically characterize baseline distributions at T0.
- **Backing Literature:** Siraz, Claes, De Castro & Vaara (2023 JMS, Table I); Thaler, Sievert, Siraz et al. (JMS Online First).

##### Q3: "What if your experiment finds a null result—does your whole thesis fail?"
- **Model Answer:** Absolutely not; a null result is theoretically profound. It identifies an empirical boundary condition for entrepreneurship policy: proving that founder-side capability training cannot cross the evaluator boundary. As Claes et al. (2025) and Siraz et al. (2026) show, evaluator bias and perceived strategy appropriateness can override founder behavior. If training fails to move external judgments, it proves public policy must shift from founder training to evaluator-facing certification platforms.
- **Backing Literature:** Claes, Siraz et al. (2025 EMR); Siraz, Claes & Alvarez (2026 JBE); Bitektine & Haack (2015 AMJ).

##### Q4: "What are your 5 training dimensions, and what does the intervention train?"
- **Model Answer:** Our 12-hour intervention trains **5 distinct legitimacy dimensions** (as established in our training design):
  - **1. Cognitive:** Clear positioning and category framing of the venture
  - **2. Pragmatic:** Demonstrating concrete value creation and client utility
  - **3. Social:** Building networks, ties, and social ease/vouching
  - **4. Factual:** Documenting expertise, track records, and operational milestones
  - **5. Regulative:** Regulatory compliance that goes beyond baseline legal minimums
  - *Theoretical Link:* These 5 trained dimensions operationalize Suchman's (1995) classical macro-dimensions by equipping founders with the specific behavioral signaling repertoires needed to generate external validity cues.
- **Backing Literature:** Mark Suchman (1995 AMR); Zimmerman & Zeitz (2002 AMR); Greg Fisher (2020 Org Theory); Alex Bitektine (2011 AMR).

#### B. MEASUREMENT — "isn’t it all self-report?"

##### Q5: "Your dependent variable relies on founder self-report. Isn't that circular?"
- **Model Answer:** We explicitly acknowledge that our survey captures the founder-side node of Schoon's (2022) relational dyad. To ensure methodological rigor, we include objective stakeholder validation milestones, and our design incorporates two direct validation extensions: applying Ghods et al.'s (2026) computational text-analysis scoring to participants' written pitch decks, and executing a Siraz-style evaluator conjoint experiment in Article 4.
- **Backing Literature:** Eric Schoon (2022 ASR); Ghods, Ricard, Aldebert & Schoon (2026 BJM); Daisy Bertrand et al. (2025 HAL).

##### Q6: "Why use Bertrand's Self-Legitimacy scale instead of standard General Self-Efficacy?"
- **Model Answer:** General self-efficacy measures a generalized belief in one's capability to execute tasks. In contrast, Bertrand's self-legitimacy instrument measures the entrepreneur's internalized feeling of social rightness, appropriateness, and entitlement to operate as a venture founder. As Bertrand, Benamara & Aldebert (2025) prove, self-illegitimacy specifically triggers impostor syndrome and suppresses action, making it the precise psychological mediator for our causal chain.
- **Backing Literature:** Daisy Bertrand, Benamara & Aldebert (2025 HAL); Alex Bitektine (2011 AMR); Michael Tost (2011 AMR).

##### Q7: "How do you defend your construct validity against Common-Method Variance (CMV)?"
- **Model Answer:** We employ procedural and statistical remedies: (1) Temporal separation between Baseline (T0), Post-treatment (T1), and Follow-up (T4); (2) Separating self-legitimacy items from external stakeholder validation scales; and (3) Triangulating self-reports against objective textual artifacts scored via Ghods et al.'s (2026) multi-dimensional NLP method.
- **Backing Literature:** Podsakoff et al. (2003 JAP); Ghods, Ricard, Aldebert & Schoon (2026 BJM).

#### C. ECOSYSTEM & BIAS — "what if bias wins anyway?"

##### Q8: "Is atEnA itself legitimate enough to confer legitimacy to its participants?"
- **Model Answer:** Aldebert's (2021, 2023) research demonstrates that support structures must establish their own institutional legitimacy before they can confer it. In our field RCT, atEnA's program-level standing and institutional backing (CCIs, regional universities) are held strictly constant between treatment and control cohorts, allowing us to isolate the specific incremental value-add of the 12h educational curriculum.
- **Backing Literature:** Bénédicte Aldebert (2021, 2023 AEI); Aldebert (2024 E&I).

##### Q9: "How do you know your 5 capability categories won't backfire?"
- **Model Answer:** We do not assume they will succeed ex-ante. Claes, Siraz et al. (2025) empirically demonstrated that legitimation strategies favored by an industry can be perceived as inappropriate by evaluators and actively backfire. That is precisely why our study is designed as an empirical field experiment: to test whether training these capabilities produces positive, neutral, or negative effects on evaluator judgments.
- **Backing Literature:** Claes, Siraz, De Castro & Lapeyre (2025 EMR); Siraz, Claes & De Castro (2026 EMR).

##### Q10: "Won't structural bias (gender, ethnicity) override any training effect?"
- **Model Answer:** Siraz et al. (2020, 2026) demonstrate that women, immigrants, and ethnic minorities face persistent baseline legitimacy penalties. This is why our empirical analysis plan incorporates formal subgroup moderation: to test whether capability training can partially compensate for structural identity deficits, or whether evaluator bias imposes an insurmountable ceiling.
- **Backing Literature:** Sonia Siraz et al. (2020 AOM); Siraz, Claes & Alvarez (2026 JBE).

##### Q11: "How do you account for institutional differences between France and Italy?"
- **Model Answer:** Ghods, Ricard & Aldebert (2024) show that social representations of the 'legitimate entrepreneur' vary across operating environments. Because norms, values, and beliefs (NVBs) differ across the Franco-Italian border, we model geographic site as a formal multi-group moderator rather than pooling cross-border data into a single homogeneous bucket.
- **Backing Literature:** Ghods, Ricard & Aldebert (2024 E&RD); Siraz et al. (2023 JMS).

##### Q12: "Why did you choose a 12-hour module instead of a multi-month incubator program?"
- **Model Answer:** A focused 12-hour intervention provides high experimental fidelity and clean causal identification. In long, multi-month incubation programs, treatments become confounded with peer effects, ad-hoc mentoring, and external market shocks. A 12-hour structured curriculum allows us to cleanly isolate the direct causal impact of codified legitimation training on entrepreneurial self-perception and signaling behavior.
- **Backing Literature:** McKenzie & Woodruff (2014 World Bank); Anderson et al. (2018 Management Science); Greg Fisher (2020 Org Theory).
